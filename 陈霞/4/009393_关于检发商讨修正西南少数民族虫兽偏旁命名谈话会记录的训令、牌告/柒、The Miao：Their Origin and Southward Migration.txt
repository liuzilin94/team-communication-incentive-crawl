IV
The Wuling Man had ravaged the southern administrative regions of
the Han Empire in 47 A.D., the Emperor sent at first general Liu Shang
to suppress them, but failed. Two years later, general Ma Yuan and others
sailed up the Yuan River and subjugated the Man in the vicinity of the
present-day Luch'i Hsien. In the years of 151, 157, and 186, the Wuling
Man uprisings were suppressed by the magistrates of local governments.
After the fall of the Han Dynasty, China was divided into three
Kingdoms (220-280） in the third century. The division of the Northern
and Southern Dynasties following the Western Chin (265-316) in the later
three centuries,created a situation for the Man-occupied lands where
political control was loose and chaotic. By T'ang times (618-906), the Man
territory was restrained by organizing administrative districts.In the
turmoil following the T'ang Dynasty known as the Five Dynasties period
(907-960). Ma Yin proclaimed himself king of a new state called Ch'u (one
of the Ten Independent States of this period) and subjugated the Man. The
Sung Dynasty (960-1279) took vigorous steps in opening up the Southern
Man Territory resulted in the sinicization of the Man that were willing to
submit, but the ‘unsubdued were put to flight into the territories of central
andwesternKweichowas far as easternYunnan on the southwest and
into the areas of Lingnan in Kwangtung and Kwangsi on the south.
The above account indicades that many of the ethnic groups of the
Wuling Man, including the Miao, Yao, Lao, Chuang and Keh-lao as first
pointed out by Yeh Ch'ien, had been forced by the Han-Chinese pressure to
migrate away from their stronghold of western Hunan and eastern Kweichow
toward south and southwestward directions. In view of their present distribu-
tion: the Miao in the regions as stated in the beginning of this paper; the
Yao in Kwangsi, northern Kwangtung, southwestern Hunan, and south
eastern Yunnan; the Lao or Keh-lao (Ruey: 1948) in Kweichow, western
Hunan, southern Szechuan, eastern Yunnan; and the Chuang·or Tai in
Kwangsi, western Kwangtung, southeastern Yunnan; it seems very likely
that the Miao (under the collective term Man） had been the dominant group
and so held good over western Hunan and eastern Kweichow ever since the
Han periods. Because of the frequent military operations of the Yuan and Ming
Dynasties in the south and Southwest, the Miao were forced to migrate
further south and southwestward resulted in their post-Sung Dynasty
sinicization and distribution.In the southward direction they cameto north-
ern Kwangsi in small numbers during the Yuan and in larger numbers
during the Ming. In the southwestward direction they reached eastern
Yunnan during the Ming. In 1381 Emperor Hungwu (1368-1398) appointed
general Fu Yu-teh to lead an army to conquer Yunnan which had reverted
tothe independent rule of local chieftains after the collapse of the Yuan
Dynasty.Fu entered Kweichow from Hunan along the Yuan River which
is the same route Ma Yuan subjugated the Wuling Man in the first
century as statedabove.Itisreadilyunderstandable thatwith general Fu's
large army burdening the area, many Miao were compelled to migrate toward

The sum of the foregoing description leads us to see the historical fact
that, the Miao,known to us so far, were originally inhabiting the midwest of
China proper in the last quarter of the third millenniumB.C.Some groups had
been banished to the northwest, some others fled out of mainland China to
the South Seas, and the remainder scattered about, more than four thousand
years ago. Later on, they appeared under th3 name of Mao in the Classic of
History during the time when King Wu subjugated the Shang or Yin Dynasty in
the twelfth century B.C. and in the Classic of Odes during the eighth century
B.C.They had been oppressed by the Ch'u and the Ch'in bureaucracies from
the eleventh down to the third century B.C. Since then the designation Miao
was hidden under the collective names of Man, Man-Yi, or Nan-Man, they
have migrated in course of time under the constant pressures of the Han-
Chinese toward the south and southwest. As a result, they were mostly found
in the regions southwest of Lake Tung-t'ing and south of the Upper Yangtze.
Most probably the Miao were the dominant ethnic group or groups among
the Wuling or Wu-ch'i Man that concentrated eventually in the regions of
present-day western Hunan,eastern Kweichow and southeastern Szechuan
provinces where they established their stronghold in post-Ch'in periods.
Since the Sung Dynasty (960-1279) the designation Miao reappeared as a
specific ethnic group. A great number of them were found moved away from
their stronghold of western Hunan, eastern Kweichow and Southeastern Sze-
chwan to the fringe-lands of Kweichow, Yunnan, and Szechuan on the west
and to Kwangsi and Yunnan on the south during theYuan(1206-1368),Ming
(1368-1643), and Ch'ing (1644-1911) periods. Froin thence they have

Peninsuladuring the last century.
When we look at the long history of southward migration of the Miao,
a question comes to mind: Why have the Miao migrated toward the south?
The answer, of course, is manifold. In the first place, one might say that
the constant Han-Chinese military opperations on and political control of the
Man, Man-Yi or Nan-Man peoples among whom the Miao, Yao, Chuang,
Lolo, Lao or Kelao are the prominant ethnic groups, have resulted in the
southward migration of the Miao as well.as other ethnic groups. It is true,
to a certain extent,because the southward march of the Han-Chinese affected
the migration ofof the non-Han-peoples.
Nevertheless, migrations are primarily caused by expulsions, or pressures
(Haddon:1911,p.1).It is a sort of driving forces which are called the
"pressure pulsations" by Eickstedt (cited in Wiens: 1954, p. 169). There

desert lands and acting upon the population of the pressure chambers of
north China, i. e., the Yellow River Valley, generating dynamic overflows
that in turn move southward into the better pressure chambers of central
China, i.e., the Yangtze River Valley. Here the inflows create pressures
which in turn send forth dynamic streams southward.and southwestward
into the lesser pressure chambers of south and southwest China, i.e.，the
Si River Valley and the smaller basins of the.Yunnan-Kweichow Plateau.
From the latter, the driving forces spill over into the unexploited Indo-
Chinese Peninsula. In short, the Han-Chinese have been forced to migrate
from time to time under the successive nomadic invasions of the northern
desert lands toward the south. In his China's March Toward the Tropics,
Wiens rightly pointed out that Eickstedt traced the Han-Chinese migrations
anthropologically in the superimposition of racial characteristics and cultural
features by northern groups successively over southern groups and reached
the conclusion that this dynamic process is the historical story of the sinici-
zation of Eastern Asia(Wiens:1954,p.169).
Although thecauscs of themigrations are complcx anddifferentfor
different areas,the Miao as well as other minoritygroups were affected
inevitably to move toward the south, to a certain extent, in the same way,
but under the constant suppression of northern and central china toward
the lesser pressure chambers of mountainous regions of southwestern China.
From there, the driving forces also spill over into the sparsely populated
northern parts of the Indo-Chinese Peninsula. Thus, we are able to see that
what is trueof theHan-Chinese is also true ofthe Miao aswell asother
ethnic groups,who have been forced to undergo the same dynamic process
in their southward migrations.
Having drawn the primary factor of driving forces above, we must
further note the secondary factor of inducing forces or atractions causing
peoples to move from one area into another. The dynamics of attractions has
dense in the Yangtze area for much surplus in central China after the two
major mass migrations in Chinese history during upheavals in the Yungchia
(307-312 A.D.） period of the Western Chin （265-316 A.D.） and in the
Chin-k'ang (1126-1127） period of Northern Sung (960-1127), the potentially
productive but sparsely settled river valleys south of the Yangtze and small
basins of the Southwest Plateau are indeed the attractions that wait to be
exploited by the land-hungry Han-Chinese. Thus the Han-Chinese in a
situation of economic pressures were induced to move in progressive stages

from the Yangtze Valley southward and southwestward to the regions
previously occupied by the Miao and other non-Han peoples that resulted in the
interspersing of the former among the latter at first.In further stages, the
latter under the vigorous pressures of the former, were forced to migrate to
where the unfavorable environments of humid, hot basins or high, steep
mountains promised to deter the rapid advance of the Han-Chinese.
However, war and politics or expulsions and attractions are not the only
impulses toward migration. A craving for more land or a seeking for better
settlement is only a partial explanation; sentiment, and a reaction against even
the slightest of social restraints had a great deal to do with it.The movemcnts
of peoples which are sufficiently dramatic for the ordinary historian to
